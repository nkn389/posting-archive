The Position:

Demographic change occurs through shifts in population size, composition (by age and sex), and territorial distribution and manifests, for example, in population ageing, youth bulge, changing household structure, distinct migration patterns, rural depopulation and rapid urbanization. These dynamics are not merely outcomes of development; they are powerful drivers. They influence economic growth and transitions, labor markets, fiscal systems and the demand for and sustainability of social services like pensions, education, healthcare and employment. They can affect inequalities and social cohesion. And they interact with climate change in affecting our planet. The relationships are bidirectional. Socio-economic conditions, peace and stability, housing, access to health and education and employment, just to name a few, significantly determine morbidity and mortality, the realization or non-realization of fertility aspirations and migration flows.

Amongst the questions linked to demographic shifts to which governments need to provide answers are the ones that relate to the future demand for public goods and services - like education, health, social protection, employment, physical infrastructure etc. - and the evolution of fiscal space. Population size, demographic composition and territorial distribution in fact shape the global and local demand for goods and services, and fiscal revenues and public spending. Through the fourth outcome of its Strategic Plan, 2026-2029, UNFPA seeks to support member states towards evidence-based and rights-based adaptation to their demographic trajectories.

Part of UNFPA’s approach is support to member states in undertaking demographic resilience reviews. These reviews entail a comprehensive forecast of the demand for various public goods and services - education, health, social protection, labor market and other public goods and services - based on projected changes in the total population and the population age structure within a longer time horizon (e.g. up to 50 years). The forecasts include baseline estimates (applying current and/or historical ratios for various public goods and services (e.g. teacher/pupil ratio) and alternative estimates assuming defined levels of improvement (based, for example, on national development visions, SDG targets and other nationally-determined targets). The demand forecasts are applied further to estimate the fiscal and revenue implications of changing population age structure, taking into consideration gender, regional and other disparities.

Country Office is seeking the services of a national consultant to contribute to a multi-country activity as part of an interregional effort by UNFPA regional offices for Eastern Europe and Central Asia (EECA) and Arab States (AS) to support evidence-based policies for adaptation to demographic change: (1) demographic resilience reviews; and (2) foresight-based scenario planning in selected countries. On demographic resilience review, the national consultant will work closely and in a complementary manner with an international consultant leading the work in selected countries. The results of the demographic resilience reviews will feed into a complementary scenario-based foresight analysis to broaden the range of probable and plausible scenarios of future demand for and supply of public services and goods taking into consideration other relevant factors and strengthen countries’ long-term planning. The national consultant will also support this part of the activity. A standard methodological approach will be developed and used across the two regions and the selected countries.

You would be responsible for:

The demographic resilience reviews will be based primarily on secondary data. The national consultant will support the analysis in close coordination with the international consultant. The foresight-based scenario planning part of the work will be in support of a second international consultant. The analysis will address the following questions for each of the selected countries:

- How will future demand for public goods and services, like education, health, social protection, pensions etc., evolve with projected changes in the size, age structure and territorial distribution (e.g. rural vs. urban) of the population? In other words, how many more/less doctors, nurses, hospital beds, teachers, schools, childcare places, etc. will be needed in 20-50 years to respond to the needs of the population?

- What will happen to public budgets and fiscal balances as the population size and structure change? In other words, how will the projected demographic shifts affect the government revenues (through changes in the working-age population and the dependency ratios, and the fiscal base) and expenditures (strongly driven by age-specific population groups)?

- How might other major factors or shifts in the horizon, for example, in the technological, social, political, environmental and other spheres, affect the demand for and the supply of public goods and services? In particular, how may technological progress (e.g. digitalization and AI) and its uptake (e.g. telemedicine, virtual schooling, etc.) impact the provision of public goods and services? How can these shifts be integrated with demographic change for a more comprehensive (foresight-based) long-term planning of public goods and services?

- How might the forecasts be influenced by and in turn influence gender, regional and other disparities?
